Significance
 Even in the absence of 
pathologies, human brains are 
characterized by age-related 
changes in structure, which 
eventually become so 
pronounced that a decrease in 
overall brain size is apparent. 
These changes may underlie 
uniquely human susceptibility to 
neurodegenerative diseases late 
in life. The evolutionary origins of 
this vulnerability remain poorly 
understood. We approach this 
problem by examining age￾related changes in brain weight 
in our closest primate relatives, 
the great apes (chimpanzees, 
bonobos, gorillas, and 
orangutans). We did not observe 
significant decrease in brain 
weight with age in these species. 
This indicates that the reduction 
in brain mass that is typical of 
normal human aging is not 
present in great apes, potentially 
linked to our species’ elevated 
risk for neurodegenerative 
conditions.
Reduction in brain weight is a hallmark feature of human aging, which is exacerbated 
in pathologies, such as Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias. However, it remains 
unclear whether a decrease in brain weight with age reflects a vulnerability unique to 
the human lineage or represents a consequence of an extension of aging processes shared 
with other primates. To address this question, we examined age- related changes in 
brain weight across our closest living relatives, the great apes (chimpanzees, bonobos, 
gorillas, and orangutans). The sample spanned the full adult age range in each species 
and included individuals reaching ages comparable to those at which age- related brain 
changes become apparent in humans. Across species, brain weight did not show signif￾icant change throughout adulthood and into advanced age. The observed pattern held 
even when body weight was included in the analysis in a subsample of chimpanzees. 
Overall, these results indicate that marked brain weight loss is not a general feature of 
great ape aging and instead appears largely specific to humans, with implications for 
understanding susceptibility to late- life brain pathology.

 Brain aging in humans represents an evolutionary conundrum, as a key feature of human 
life history, namely a long lifespan, can compromise neural systems that support cognitive 
function with age. Even in the absence of disease, normal human aging is accompanied 
by cognitive changes (reviewed in 1), including alterations in information processing 
(2–4) and shifts in brain organization compared to younger individuals. Research has 
shown that aging in humans involves a decrease in cerebral volume (5, 6) in the gray 
matter (GM) and white matter (WM; 7, 8), reduction in the size of specific cortical areas 
and subcortical structures (9–11), and various microscopic changes, such as decreased 
complexity of cortical pyramidal neuron dendritic arbors and lower density of synaptic 
spines (12, 13). The extent to which such age-related changes are also present in other 
primates is an open question, as brain aging in nonhuman primates displays variation
among structures that appear species-specific and distinct from patterns observed in 
humans (14–16). Distinguishing the features associated with aging shared with other 
primate species from those that are uniquely human represents a crucial step in our 
understanding of the factors that make certain brain regions (17–20) or classes of neurons 
(21–23) prone to selective degeneration, leading human brains to be especially vulnerable 
to pathologies in later life. 
 Decrease in overall brain size represents one feature that might be unique to aging in 
humans among primates. Both in vivo MRI studies of brain volume and postmortem 
autopsy studies of brain weight demonstrate a progressive decline in overall brain size in 
humans from adulthood into old age. In an extensive review of more than 50 MRI lon￾gitudinal studies, Hedman et al. (24) reported a 0.2% per year decrease in overall brain 
volume from 30 years of age onward, which, after the age of 60 increases to 0.5% of 
volume loss per year. Focusing specifically on aged individuals (59 y and older), Resnick 
et al. (25) reported a decrease of 5.4 cm3
 per year in brain volume on average. However, 
it is important to note potential differences in the rate of brain volume decrease across 
human populations. For example, it has been reported that the rate of decrease in brain 
volume among Tsimane, a forager/horticulturalist population in lowland Bolivia, is lower 
compared to European and US populations (26), highlighting the role of diet, activity, 
exposure to pathogens, and other environmental factors on brain aging. 
 Postmortem studies of human brain weight typically report lower values for change 
with age, although the pattern of the gradual decline remains 2.7 g per year in males and 
2.2 g per year in females (27) that increases to 5 g per year after the age of 80 y (28). An 
often-cited study (29) utilizing a large sample of over 4,000 autopsies reported a gradual 
decrease in brain weight starting between 45 and 50 years of age. Quantifying the decrease

 There are few data on aging-related changes in brain size among 
humans’ closest living primate relatives, the great apes (chimpan￾zees, bonobos, gorillas, and orangutans). The evolutionary split 
between humans and the other great apes occurred approximately 
6 to 8 million years ago (Mya; (33), which is considerably more 
recent than between humans and cercopithecoid monkeys, includ￾ing macaques (30 Mya; 34). Importantly, great apes have substan￾tially shorter lifespans than humans, raising the question of 
whether age-related brain atrophy is a uniquely human trait linked 
to our extended postreproductive longevity (35, 36). This com￾parison is essential for placing human brain aging in evolutionary 
context. A previous study employing a sample of 76 chimpanzees 
(Pan troglodytes ) including individuals in their 40s and 50s, 
reported a decrease in brain weight of 1.1 g per year (37). However, 
when males and females were analyzed separately, the decrease in 
brain weight was not statistically significant. These findings suggest 
that if brain weight declines with aging in chimpanzees at all, it 
may be well below the amount reported for humans. 
 In the present study, we analyzed brain weight changes with 
age in four great ape taxa utilizing a sample that covers the adult 
lifespan. The dataset is the largest and most complete of its kind, 
which allowed us to: 1) revisit the extent of changes in brain weight 
throughout the lifespan in chimpanzees, by including a signifi￾cantly larger sample with more geriatric specimens compared to 
previous studies, and 2) determine whether age-related decline in 
brain weight is found in other great ape species, namely bonobos 
(Pan paniscus ), western lowland gorillas (Gorilla gorilla ), and oran￾gutans (Pongo pygmaeus and Pongo abelii ). 
Results

males separately in each species due to sexual dimorphism and 
differences in lifespan where females represented a larger propor￾tion of the oldest individuals (Table 1). The sample included sub￾jects beginning at 4 years of age, which is when total brain size in 
great apes typically approaches adult-like levels (Materials and 
Methods ). The substantial variation in brain weight observed 
within species is consistent with previously reported variation in 
great ape endocranial volume, where the largest individuals may 
be approximately twice the cranial capacity of the smallest adults 
within the same species (38). To assess the relationship between 
brain weight and age, we fit least squares linear regressions for each

slope. Slopes that had 95% CIs which included zero were consid￾ered to indicate stable brain weight with age. To evaluate potential 
nonlinear age-related patterns, we first tested quadratic age models 
against linear alternatives for each species and sex using the Akaike 
Information Criterion (AIC). In no case did quadratic models 
improve fit (all ΔAIC < 2), and linear models were therefore 
retained for parsimony. We additionally conducted objective

Discussion
 Across the great apes, we found that brain weight does not signif￾icantly decrease from the age at which adult brain size is attained 
into old age. In comparison, humans exhibit marked reductions 
in brain size beginning between 45 to 50 years of age and accel￾erate thereafter (27–31). The differences between humans as 
reported in the literature (27–29) and the great apes analyzed here 
is unlikely to be driven by variation in the ages represented in the 
samples, as a sizable number of geriatric specimens over the age 
of 50 y were included (14 gorillas, 5 orangutans, and 42 chim￾panzees). The only species with a relatively small proportion of 
older individuals was bonobos, where only 2 of 13 adult specimens 
were over the age of 50 y. Although sample sizes differed substan￾tially among species, particularly for bonobos and orangutans, the 
results were consistent across all great apes examined. Future stud￾ies with larger samples will be important for refining species￾specific estimates of age-related change and for detecting subtle 
effects that may not be apparent in the currently available datasets. 
Notably, these results are based on individuals with a wide range 
of causes of death, encompassing older great apes known to exhibit 
age-related neuropathology such as phosphorylated tau and amy￾loid β accumulation (39). Therefore, the absence of detectable

is remarkable, suggesting that overall brain mass is relatively pre￾served despite the likely presence of such pathology in some indi￾viduals. In future work, it will be important to compare trajectories 
of brain aging between individuals with and without neuropatho￾logical changes; however, subdividing the sample in this manner 
will require substantially larger datasets to achieve sufficient sta￾tistical power for such comparisons. 
 As with any cross-sectional study, our analyses may underesti￾mate age-related change if individuals with relatively smaller brains 
or accelerated brain aging are less likely to survive into older age 
classes. Such selective disappearance could attenuate the appear￾ance of age-related trends by causing older individuals to represent 
a nonrandom subset of the population. However, evaluating this 
possibility directly requires longitudinal measurements of brain 
size within individuals. Although longitudinal neuroimaging stud￾ies have been instrumental in characterizing human brain aging, 
comparable datasets are unlikely to become available for great apes 
because repeated MRI examinations are constrained by ethical 
and practical limitations. Nevertheless, the absence of detectable 
age-related decline in brain weight observed here contrasts with 
both cross-sectional autopsy and longitudinal MRI studies in 
humans, which demonstrate measurable reductions in brain size 
with advancing age. Thus, although survivorship effects cannot 
be excluded, they are unlikely to fully account for the difference 
currently observed between humans and other great apes. 
 Our findings indicating a lack of significant age-related brain 
weight changes in a large sample of chimpanzees differ from a 
previous report in which males and females were analyzed together 
(37). However, as female brain weights are generally lower than 
males, this apparent relationship was likely driven by a higher 
proportion of females surviving into old age, which is consistent 
with our data (Fig. 1A and Table 2). 
 Differences in the patterns of aging between humans and other 
primates are typically attributed to a prolonged life span. In a study 
examining cortical aging in humans and chimpanzees (15), it was 
noted that the structures displaying differences—frontal WM, 
neocortical WM, and the hippocampus—started decreasing in 
elderly humans older than the oldest chimpanzees included in that 
analysis. A separate study similarly did not find changes in overall 
GM and cortical thickness associated with age in chimpanzees 
(40). Recent comparative neuroimaging analyses further indicate 
that the spatial pattern of age-related gray matter decline in humans 
is strongly associated with regions that experienced the greatest 
evolutionary cortical expansion, particularly in higher-order pre￾frontal areas, whereas chimpanzees do not show this relationship 
(18). This finding suggests that brain regions that expanded most 
during recent human evolution may also be disproportionately 
vulnerable to aging-related atrophy. The oldest chimpanzees in 
these studies were in their 40s, that is, past the typical life expec￾tancy of around 30 for chimpanzees in the wild (41) and at the 
upper range of those reported for captive chimpanzees (42). In 
humans, many of the traits associated with brain aging display little 
change until well into old age; cerebral WM, for example, does not 
start showing noticeable decline until the age of 60 (7, 8). 
On the other hand, changes in overall brain weight of humans 
have an earlier onset, between 45 and 50 years of age (29, 31). The 
great apes analyzed in the current study included specimens within 
that age range, and a significant proportion was older than 50. 
Recent evidence indicates that chimpanzees (43) and mountain 
gorillas (44) can reach advanced postreproductive ages even in the 
wild, reinforcing the need for comparative studies that sample 
individuals across the full lifespan to disentangle the contributions 
of longevity and species-specific aging processes. The current 
findings support the hypothesis that the evolution of extreme 
human longevity may amplify the cumulative effects of brain
aging, leading to the more pronounced atrophy observed in 
humans. This effect may be further accentuated in contemporary 
human populations, where advances in nutrition, public health, 
and medical care have substantially increased survival into 
extremely old ages that were likely uncommon throughout much 
of human evolutionary history. As a result, many individuals now 
live long enough to experience neurobiological changes that may 
have had limited opportunity to manifest in ancestral populations 
or in other great ape species with shorter average lifespans. 
 In nonhuman primates, age-related microstructural changes in 
the brain, such as synaptic and dendritic regression, alterations in 
white matter, and localized cortical thinning are well documented 
(reviewed in ref. 39), yet these rarely culminate in the substantial, 
measurable reductions in total brain weight that are typical of 
aging humans. Our findings reinforce this distinction: great apes 
showed no evidence of significant age-related brain weight loss. 
Together, these findings suggest that absolute longevity in humans, 
combined with species-specific vulnerabilities such as vascular and 
metabolic diseases, may allow the cumulative effects of micro￾structural aging to manifest as widespread atrophy and brain 
weight loss. It remains to be explored how other aspects of somatic 
aging common to great apes and humans, including cerebral vas￾cular disease, decrease in sensory abilities and mobility (45), relate 
to the unique patterns of brain aging across species. 
Materials and Methods
Subjects and Weight Measurements. Data on brain weight were collected 
from necropsy records provided by zoos and primate research institutions, as 
well as weight measurements acquired upon arrival of donated specimens to 
the National Chimpanzee Brain Resource (www.chimpanzeebrain.org) and Great 
Ape Neuroscience Project repositories. Only specimens with known age and sex 
were used in the analysis. If weight for only one hemisphere was available, the 
values were doubled to get an estimate of the total weight. Although great 
apes exhibit population- level hemispheric asymmetries, MRI studies indicate 
that these differences are modest relative to total cerebral volume (46), making 
hemisphere doubling a reasonable approximation of total brain weight. Part of 
the chimpanzee sample was previously used in Herndon et al.’s (37) study of 
brain weight with age in chimpanzees.
The sample consisted of 51 orangutans (Pongo spp.), 77 western lowland 
gorillas (Gorilla gorilla), 16 bonobos (Pan paniscus), and 457 chimpanzees (Pan 
troglodytes). The captive chimpanzee population in North America is derived 
predominantly from founders of the Western chimpanzee subspecies (Pan trog￾lodytes verus), which contributed approximately 95% of the ancestry of the US 
captive population (47). Given the low number of specimens in the orangutan 
sample, the specimens representing Bornean (Pongo pygmaeus), Sumatran 
(Pongo abelii), and hybrids were analyzed together. Specimens were included 
in the analysis regardless of the reported cause of death and neuropathologi￾cal findings, although we did exclude specimens for which the sample was too 
physically damaged from necropsy procedures to allow for reliable brain weight 
measurement. For specimens with comparatively low brain weights, the brains 
and/or photographic records were inspected to verify that the tissue was grossly 
intact and that there was no evidence of substantial tissue loss that would com￾promise the reliability of the measurement. Further details of the sample used 
in this study are provided in Table 1.
Statistical Analyses. We performed least squares linear regression to deter￾mine whether changes in brain weight were associated with age. The data were 
analyzed separately for each species, using only subjects which reached adult 
brain size, and separately for females and males. As published reports indicate 
that adult brain size in gorillas (48) and chimpanzees (49, 50) is reached at the 
age of 4 y, our analysis included individuals 4 years of age and older. Given that 
brain growth data for orangutans and bonobos, to the best of our knowledge,

Even though overall brain size in great apes reaches adult- like values at this age, 
we acknowledge that such individuals remain immature from a life history per￾spective, with continued somatic, behavioral, and neurobiological development 
occurring through juvenility and adolescence (51).
Analyses were conducted separately for males and females in gorillas, oran￾gutans, bonobos, and chimpanzees. For each group, we ­t ordinary least squares 
(OLS) linear regressions of brain weight (g) versus age (years). For each model, 
we report the slope, 95% CI, P- values, and coef­cient of determination (R²). 
Signi­cance was set at α = 0.05.
To test whether age- related changes in brain weight exhibited nonlinear 
patterns, we evaluated quadratic and piecewise alternatives to linear age mod￾els. First, we compared linear and quadratic regression models separately for 
each species and sex. Linear models included age as a single predictor, whereas 
quadratic models additionally included an age2
 term to capture potential curva￾ture in age- related trajectories. Model support was evaluated using the Akaike 
Information Criterion (AIC), with differences in AIC (ΔAIC) used to assess relative 
model ­t. When ΔAIC between quadratic and linear models was less than 2, the 
linear model was preferred for parsimony.
Second, we applied piecewise linear regression allowing a single breakpoint, 
which was objectively selected using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). 
Candidate breakpoints were evaluated across the central 80% of the observed 
age range to minimize edge effects, and piecewise models were only retained if 
they provided a substantially better ­t than a single- slope model (ΔBIC > 2). To 
reduce the likelihood of over­tting, each segment was required to include a min￾imum of six individuals. Because our hypothesis speci­cally concerned late- life 
decline, only breakpoints associated with a negative slope in the postbreakpoint 
segment were considered biologically meaningful and interpreted as evidence 
for age- related reduction in brain weight.
To examine if the variation in brain weight can be explained by differences in 
body size, we performed least squares regression on individuals 15 y or older, 
separately for female (n = 147) and male (n = 131) chimpanzees. Next, to assess 
the effect of body weight on the observed pattern of brain changes with age, we 
analyzed residual brain weight (from brain ~ body weight) in relation to age. 
Given that the information on body weight was only available for a subset of our 
chimpanzee sample, we had to limit our analysis to only one species—chimpan￾zees—and only to those individuals for which the information on body weight was 
available. However, these data need to be interpreted with caution, as almost 
one half of our female chimpanzee sample (n = 50) had body weight above 60 
kg, which has been suggested to indicate overweight captive female chimpan￾zees (52). Whereas comparable data for distinguishing overweight from normal 
weight are not available for chimpanzee males, 48 out of 94 males in our sample 
weighed more than 61 kg, which has been reported as the mean weight for 
captive male chimpanzees (52), suggesting cautious interpretation is warranted.
For a subset of specimens in each species, values were available for both 
fresh and formalin- ­xed brain weight. To examine potential influences of the 
condition of the tissue (fresh versus formalin- ­xed) on our ­ndings, we compiled 
two datasets for each species, separately for males and females. One dataset con￾sisted of brain weight measurements utilized in the analysis, based on the tissue 
condition represented by a majority of the sample. For gorillas and orangutans, 
values for most of the specimens were collected on formalin- ­xed specimens; 
for chimpanzees, measurements for most of the specimens were obtained during 
necropsy, representing weight of the brain before ­xation. In the second dataset, 
we replaced the values for the specimens for which both ­xed and fresh values 
were available with the opposite to what was included in the ­rst dataset: for 
gorillas and orangutans, we replaced the values of formalin- ­xed specimens with 
the values for fresh specimens, and for chimpanzees, the values were replaced 
with those obtained from ­xed brains. The only species for which the analysis of 
the tissue condition was not performed is bonobos, as the values for all except 
one bonobo specimen were based on formalin- ­xed specimens. Average values 
of each dataset per species are reported in SI Appendix, Table S1.
We tested whether tissue condition was confounded with age by comparing 
the age distributions of specimens for which fresh versus ­xed brain weights 
differed with those of the remaining specimens. Age distributions did not differ 
signi­cantly in any species- by- sex group (all Mann–Whitney P > 0.12). Logistic 
regression likewise showed that age did not signi­cantly predict whether a spec￾imen had a fresh versus ­xed replacement value, either within species while 
controlling for sex (chimpanzees, P = 0.071; gorillas, P = 0.564; orangutans, 
P = 0.750) or across the complete sample while controlling for species and sex 
(P = 0.118). Thus, we found no evidence that tissue condition was systematically 
confounded with age. Next, we performed linear regression to examine change 
in brain weight with age using both datasets for each species, analyzing females 
and males separately, and compared the slopes. We did not ­nd a statistically 
signi­cant difference between the slopes in any of the species (chimpanzee 
females P = 0.997, chimpanzee males P = 0.753; gorilla females P = 0.696; 
gorilla males P = 0.785; orangutan females P = 0.979, orangutan males 
P = 0.972; SI Appendix, Table S1). Thus, we concluded that the condition of 
the tissue did not influence our ­ndings, and the mixture of specimens whose 
weight was obtained using fresh and formalin- ­xed material in each species 
resulted in similar slopes.
Species and sex differences in slopes were evaluated using ANCOVA contrasts 
within each species, corrected for multiple comparisons using Tukey HSD. All 
analyses were performed in Python. Graphs were constructed using GraphPad 
Prism version 10 for Mac OS (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA).
